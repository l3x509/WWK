Screen Time Guidelines by Age: What Changed in 2026

🍿 WatchWithKids July 27, 2026 8 min read

In January 2026, the American Academy of Pediatrics quietly retired the screen time rule most parents grew up hearing — the flat two-hour daily cap. Below is the current screen time chart by age, followed by what actually changed and why.

Screen time guidelines by age — the chart

Here's the current AAP screen time recommendation for each age group, at a glance. The under-6 numbers are still concrete limits; age 6 and up moved to a quality-based framework in 2026, explained in detail below the table.

AAP screen time guidelines by age (2026)
AgeGuideline
Under 18 monthsAvoid screens entirely, except live video chatting with family
18–24 monthsIf introduced, only high-quality programming, watched together with a caregiver
2–5 yearsAbout 1 hour per day of high-quality, age-appropriate content
6+ yearsNo fixed hour limit — evaluated by content quality, viewing context, and what it displaces (sleep, activity, family time)

That "no fixed hour limit" row is the headline change. For nearly a decade, the AAP's go-to guidance for six-and-up was simple and easy to repeat: no more than two hours of recreational screen time a day. In 2026, that specific number was formally retired in favor of the three-question framework covered next.

What changed in January 2026

Parents liked the old two-hour rule because it was concrete. Researchers had been pushing back on it for years, arguing that a flat number treats a video call with a grandparent the same as two hours of fast-cut, algorithm-driven video — which is clearly not the same thing developmentally.

In January 2026, the AAP formally dropped the fixed-hour model for children six and up. The updated screen time guidelines don't ask "how many hours." They ask about quality, context, and displacement instead — a shift toward evaluating what the screen time actually consists of, rather than just measuring it on a clock.

For the youngest children, the numbers in the chart above didn't change. The guidance for under-18-months and the 2–5 age bracket remains grounded in developmental research that hasn't shifted, and it's still expressed as concrete limits rather than a judgment call.

The three questions that replaced the clock

Rather than a single number, the 2026 screen time guidelines ask parents to weigh three factors for any given stretch of screen time:

1. Quality — what is it, actually?

Is the content age-appropriate, reasonably paced, and something a child can genuinely engage with — or is it fast-cut, algorithmically optimized to maximize watch time regardless of what it's teaching or showing? A slower, story-driven film scores very differently from an endless short-form feed, even at the same runtime.

2. Context — alone, or together?

A child watching alone in a bedroom is a different situation than a child watching with a parent nearby, talking about what's happening on screen. Co-viewing and conversation are treated as meaningfully protective factors in the new guidance, not just a nice-to-have.

3. Displacement — what isn't happening instead?

The core underlying question: is this screen time coming at the cost of sleep, physical activity, family time, or unstructured play? An hour of a calm family film on a Saturday afternoon, with everyone in the room together, is not treated the same as an hour of screen time that replaces a child's only chance at outdoor play that day.

The practical takeaway

The new screen time guidelines don't mean "no limits." They mean the limits are now about the shape of the screen time, not just its length. A deliberately chosen, age-appropriate film watched together as a family checks every box the new framework cares about — which is a fairly reassuring way to think about movie night specifically.

What this means for movie night

Read against the three-question framework, an intentional family movie night comes out looking like exactly the kind of screen time the updated AAP guidelines are trying to encourage more of, not less:

  • Quality: A chosen film, appropriate to your child's age, beats an open-ended algorithmic feed on every axis the new guidelines care about.
  • Context: Watching together, with room to pause and talk, is precisely the co-viewing context the AAP flags as protective.
  • Displacement: A single film in the evening, after the day's activity and before bed, generally isn't displacing much of anything.

This doesn't mean every screen minute is equal — a three-hour marathon on a school night is still worth thinking twice about. But the shift in the screen time guidelines is genuinely good news for the specific, deliberate kind of watching this site is built around: pick something age-appropriate, watch it together, and talk about it afterward.

Frequently asked questions

What is the new AAP screen time guideline for 2026?

In January 2026, the AAP moved away from a flat two-hour daily limit for children six and up, replacing it with a framework based on the quality of the content, the context in which it's watched, and what it may be displacing — such as sleep, activity, or family time.

Did the screen time guidelines change for toddlers and preschoolers?

No — guidance for children under 18 months (avoid screens except video chatting) and ages 2–5 (about one hour of high-quality content daily) remains concrete and unchanged in the 2026 update, as shown in the chart above.

Is watching a movie together considered good screen time under the new guidelines?

Generally, yes. Co-viewing an age-appropriate film with family present addresses two of the three factors the new framework emphasizes — content quality and shared context — better than solo, algorithm-driven viewing does.

How much screen time is still okay for a 6-year-old?

There's no longer a fixed hour figure in official AAP guidance, though many pediatric sources still suggest roughly two hours a day as a reasonable starting point. The bigger factors now are what the content is, whether a parent is engaged with it, and what it's replacing in the child's day.

Is there an official screen time chart by age for 2026?

The AAP no longer publishes a single fixed-hour chart for every age group. Concrete numbers still apply under 18 months and for ages 2–5 — see the chart at the top of this guide. For age 6 and up, guidance is now based on content quality, viewing context, and what the screen time displaces, rather than a specific number of hours.

Why did the AAP drop the two-hour screen time rule?

Researchers argued a flat hour limit treated very different kinds of screen time as equivalent — a video call with a grandparent versus fast-paced, algorithm-driven video, for instance. The 2026 framework replaces the single number with three questions about quality, context, and displacement instead.
