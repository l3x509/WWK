Is Moana (2026) Appropriate for Kids?

🍿 WatchWithKids July 27, 2026 7 min read

The live-action Moana hit theaters on July 10, 2026, and if your family loved the 2016 animated original, the question isn't whether to see it — it's whether your specific kid is ready for it. Here's what's actually in it, verified against multiple parent-review sources, so you can decide before you buy tickets.

  • Why it's rated PG
  • What's different from the 2016 original

Quick verdict

Moana (2026) is rated PG and is a faithful, effects-heavy live-action remake of the 2016 animated film, directed by Thomas Kail and starring Catherine Laga'aia as Moana and Dwayne Johnson returning as Maui. Multiple family-media reviewers agree on the core takeaway: the story hasn't changed, but the peril feels more intense in live action than it did in animation, and several reviewers specifically flag it as more suitable for kids around 7 and up, with guidance recommended through about age 10.

If your child handled the 2016 animated Moana comfortably, they'll likely be fine with this one too — but go in expecting the storm sequences, the giant crab, and the lava monster to land with noticeably more weight than the cartoon versions did.

Why it's rated PG

The MPAA rating cites action/peril, some scary images, rude humor, and brief thematic elements — the same overall category as the original animated film, with "rude humor" newly added this time around.

Peril
Action & danger sequences

Moana and Maui face a series of escalating threats: a raid by the coconut-armored Kakamora, a fight with the giant crab Tamatoa, and a climactic confrontation with the lava demon Te Kā. None of it is graphic, but the live-action rendering makes these sequences feel more visceral than their animated counterparts — reviewers consistently note this as the film's biggest departure in tone from the original.

Loss
A grandmother's death

As in the original, Moana's grandmother Tala dies partway through the film and later reappears as a guiding spirit. This beat carries over from the animated version, so if your child has already seen and handled that story, this shouldn't be new territory — though it may land with more emotional weight here.

Language
Mild only

Reviewers note a handful of mild lines — a "son of a beach" pun, "sucks," "dumb," and similar schoolyard-level language. There's no strong profanity anywhere in the film.

Other
No sexual content or substance use

Reviewers confirm nothing sexual and no substance use anywhere in the film. Some island clothing is described as somewhat revealing, in line with the setting, but nothing beyond what the animated version already showed.

What's different from the 2016 original

Multiple reviews describe this as a close, scene-for-scene remake rather than a reinterpretation — the plot, structure, and most of the songs carry over directly. The core difference parents should know about is simply the medium: what read as stylized and safely cartoonish in animation now involves real actors, water, and CGI creatures rendered with photorealistic weight. A storm at sea is more frightening when it looks like a real storm. A giant lava monster is more unsettling when it's staged with realistic lighting and scale.

This is the single most consistent note across parent-review outlets: the content is nearly identical, but the intensity is turned up simply because it looks real. Families who found the 2016 film's scarier moments to be a non-issue should still expect this version to land a notch higher.

Age-by-age guidance

Under 6

Most reviewers who address this age bracket directly recommend caution. One family-media outlet specifically states the film isn't suitable for children under 7 given the scary scenes and violence, even though the rating itself is a straightforward PG. If your child is easily frightened by realistic-looking peril — as opposed to cartoon peril — consider waiting for streaming, where you can pause, skip, or watch with the lights on and a hand to hold.

Ages 7–10

This is the range most reviews converge on as the sweet spot, with guidance still recommended through around age 10. Kids in this range who already know and love the animated Moana are generally well-prepared for the story — the newness here is intensity, not content. Sitting together for the Kakamora, Tamatoa, and Te Kā sequences, ready to offer reassurance, is a reasonable plan rather than an overreaction.

11 and up

Kids at this age and older will likely find the film's themes of courage, identity, and leadership land as intended, without needing much additional support. The mild rude humor and light insults are unlikely to register as anything but normal at this age.

The bottom line

If your family already has a relationship with the 2016 animated Moana and your child handled it well, this version tells the same story — just with more visual weight behind the scary parts. For sensitive kids under 7, waiting for a home viewing where you control the environment is a reasonable call.

Frequently asked questions

Is Moana (2026) scarier than the original animated Moana?

The content is nearly identical, but multiple reviewers agree the live-action rendering makes the peril sequences — the storm, the Kakamora raid, Tamatoa, and Te Kā — feel more intense simply because they look photorealistic rather than animated.

Does anyone die in Moana (2026)?

Yes — as in the original, Moana's grandmother Tala dies during the film and later returns as a guiding spirit. This is consistent with the 2016 animated version.

Is there bad language in Moana (2026)?

No strong profanity. Reviewers note only mild schoolyard-level language, including a "son of a beach" pun and words like "sucks" and "dumb."

What age is Moana (2026) appropriate for?

The film carries a PG rating. Most family-media reviews suggest it's best suited to children around age 7 and up, with parental guidance recommended through roughly age 10 given the more intense live-action peril sequences.
